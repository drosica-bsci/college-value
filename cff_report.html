Executive Summary

The problem isn't motivation.
It's the missing navigation layer.

Each year, hundreds of thousands of working adults in California consider returning to school. Most approach the decision thoughtfully. And yet a significant share end up in programs that don't deliver on their goals — or don't enroll at all. This report explains why — and what to do about it.

What We Found

Adults are intentional and clear about what they value. The gap is not motivational — it is navigational. A broken decision environment makes it genuinely hard to act on what adults already know. We call this the decision infrastructure gap.

What This Means for Design

Effective interventions must meet adults where their constraints are, make implicit tradeoffs explicit, and embed trusted human support at the moment of decision. Four design concepts emerge as the most promising starting points.

The Four Findings

Adults know what they value, but can't predict which programs will deliver it. Structural constraints eliminate options before comparison begins. When formal signals fail, adults build workarounds — rational but unreliable.

What Comes Next

The most tractable near-term opportunity: adults who have decided to pursue education but lack support to choose well. CBOs, navigators, and institutions can act now by building decision support into existing touchpoints.

The Problem

The decision infrastructure gap

The policy conversation around adult learners has increasingly converged on a single metric: return on investment. If we can steer adults toward high-ROI programs, better decisions will follow. This instinct is understandable — but incomplete. ROI flattens individual interest and fit into a single variable. It implies the right answer is the same for everyone. And it misses the deeper structural issue.

The landscape adults are navigating has grown substantially more complex. Where previous generations largely chose between a four-year college and not going, today's learners face two-year degrees, certificates, fully online programs, hybrid formats, apprenticeships, employer-sponsored pathways, and more. This proliferation is, in principle, a good thing. In practice, it means adults are making high-stakes choices in a significantly more complicated environment — often without the tools or support to navigate it.

Compounding this is the extreme variance in outcomes across programs, even within the same field. Two adults pursuing credentials in the same broad area can end up with dramatically different earnings, employment prospects, and levels of employer recognition. That variance is often invisible at the point of decision. The information needed to see it is fragmented, non-standardized, and frequently absent.

What we found reframes the problem. Adults are not passive, unmotivated, or confused about what they want. They are deliberate. The gap is not in their goals or effort. The gap is in the infrastructure that should help them translate those goals into a well-reasoned choice — and that infrastructure is largely missing.

What We Learned

Four findings, one underlying problem

Drawn from more than 50 interviews, these findings trace a consistent story. The barriers adults face are not primarily about motivation, identity, or a failure to understand what they value. They are about a system that makes it genuinely hard to act on what adults already know.

1

Adults are intentional and clear about what they value — but what feels "worth it" shifts depending on how far ahead they can afford to look

Across every segment we interviewed, people could articulate with striking clarity what value meant to them. Higher wages or career advancement. Credentials their employer would actually recognize. Skills they could apply immediately. Stability, mobility, low financial risk. Flexibility compatible with work and caregiving. Adults were not confused about what "worth it" means. They were specific about it.

This clarity matters because it reframes where the problem lies. If adults already know what they want, the gap is not motivational — it is navigational. The challenge is not getting adults to care about outcomes. It is helping them find and evaluate the programs most likely to deliver on what they already care about.

But value is not a fixed point. What adults prioritize depends significantly on how far ahead they feel they can afford to plan. For adults facing immediate financial pressure, caregiving demands, or job instability, the horizon compresses: speed, flexibility, and low cost dominate. Precarity compresses what people plan for. A learner who says "I just need something short and cheap" may not be indifferent to long-term outcomes — she may simply not have the bandwidth to plan for them right now.

"Definitely job availability and income at the end of it. Am I able to find a job once I do finish the program, and how much would it pay?… The reason why I chose Cybersecurity was because the salary outcome outweighed IT, so that's what kind of made my final decision."
— Prospective student, associate's program
"I work full-time, I'm a full-time mom and wife… I don't have a lot of time to go through the material. I decided to enroll in it because it's not a long program."
— Student, certificate program
"Whether a program is worth it really depends on what fulfills you. Even if the pay isn't great or there's not much growth, if the work makes you happy and you feel connected to the people you work with, that matters."
— Prospective student, associate's program
2

Adults struggle to predict which programs will actually deliver on what they value — and the system gives them almost nothing reliable to work with

Despite knowing what they value, adults consistently struggled to answer the questions that matter most: Will employers actually recognize this credential? What do graduates from this specific program earn? What additional steps will be required after completion? These are not obscure questions. They are the basic inputs to a reasonable decision. And for most adults navigating most programs, they are effectively unanswerable.

The information environment is deeply fragmented. Programs report different metrics. Wage data, where it exists, is typically averaged at the field or institution level — not the program level where it would be most useful. Employer recognition is implied through marketing rather than verified through evidence. There is no standardized format, no way to compare programs on what matters.

In our second round of interviews, we introduced eight prospective learners to College Scorecard, Career Explorer, and Niche. Not one had heard of any of them. Once introduced, most reacted positively and said they would use them. The problem is not that adults are uninterested in better information. It is that the tools are effectively invisible at the moment of decision.

"There's especially not universal [data about outcomes]. If you know who to ask at the school, they may or may not have it… But it's just very high-level percentages, not enough detail that if you were shelling out thousands of dollars to be like, 'Okay, this is the one for me.'"
— Recent graduate, online certificate program
"I think I would have to say almost like a massive table of expectations. When you're deciding on a program… what those employment options look like in terms of 5, 10 years from now… I think something like that would help massively."
— Student, apprenticeship program
"The construction program had four different options. They had a chart as to the expected timeline, as well as the salary increases per year… and they had that lined up for you."
— Student, apprenticeship program
3

Structural constraints don't narrow choices — they eliminate them

For most working adults, the decision process doesn't begin with a survey of options. It begins with the rapid elimination of everything that won't work. Work schedules rule out anything with fixed in-person hours. Caregiving responsibilities eliminate programs requiring full-time intensity. Transportation constraints remove distant campuses. Finances make higher-cost programs unavailable, regardless of their quality.

The result: most adults are not choosing between "best" and "better." They are choosing between possible and impossible. By the time any real evaluation begins, the viable choice set may already be reduced to one or two programs. The real bottleneck is not at the comparison stage — it is upstream, at the point where options are being considered or ruled out at all.

Cognitive load compounds the problem. Adults searching for programs are often doing so at the margins of already-full lives — at night after work, navigating program websites between caregiving. Under these conditions, structured comparison becomes less likely. Heuristic shortcuts fill the gap.

"I'm looking at Riverside and PCC… since I'm still having a bit of car problems and stuff, that just makes it more convenient [to be remote]… For right now, just due to my situation, it'll have to be mostly remote."
— Prospective student, associate's program
"CSULA is almost like a default no-brainer… it's the closest school, it's the easiest one to get to, but CSUN has some programs that are more interesting to me."
— Prospective student, bachelor's program
"I didn't look at other schools much. I knew [my program] was free and online — that's what mattered."
— Recent graduate, online certificate program
4

When formal signals fail, adults build workarounds — and the results are mixed

Adults don't give up when the formal information system fails them. They build workarounds: Reddit threads, coworkers who went through something similar, informal networks that traffic in lived experience. For many, these are the primary decision infrastructure — the most useful information they encountered in the entire process.

Informal channels do real things well. They provide practical insight that formal sources rarely offer — what the workload actually feels like, what employers in a specific city think of a credential, what the hidden steps are that a program website glosses over. But they are inconsistent in ways that matter: anecdotes substitute for data, success stories are overrepresented, and mismatches cluster predictably where formal signals are weakest.

Adults trust institutional sources and knowledgeable people far more than informal channels. But those sources are rarely available when decisions are actually being made. Adults are not choosing informal channels because they prefer them. They are defaulting to them because the more trusted alternatives are effectively out of reach.

"I would go to Reddit and Quora… those options made it really easy for me to figure out and decide."
— Prospective student, bachelor's program
"You go onto the website, of course it's going to be like, we offer [this and this]… but that's just fluff, right?"
— Prospective student, bachelor's program
"There's no summary table comparing this program to this program… expected salary, cost… I just try to do it on my own."
— Student, apprenticeship program

What This Means for Design

Four concepts grounded in the diagnosis

Effective interventions in this space need to meet adults where their constraints actually are, make implicit tradeoffs explicit, and embed trusted human support at the right moment. These four concepts are not finished solutions — they are grounded, testable articulations of what a navigation layer for adult learners could look like in practice.

Meet constraints where they are

Solutions that assume a wide-open choice set or easy access to advisors will miss the majority of people this work is trying to reach.

Make tradeoffs explicit

Adults have clear values but limited ability to predict which programs will honor them. Surfacing what programs will actually deliver addresses a persistent gap.

Embed trusted human support

Adults trust institutional sources and knowledgeable people. But those sources are rarely available when decisions are being made. Closing that gap is high-leverage.

Downstream · Midstream

Guided Choice Sessions

A structured, human-supported session that helps an adult work through their decision at the moment it matters most — not an information dump, but a facilitated process that surfaces constraints, clarifies tradeoffs, and produces a concrete next step.

Behavioral mechanism

Simplification and structure at the decision moment. Reduces choice overload. Human support creates accountability that unstructured self-directed research rarely produces.

Insight 1 Insight 2 Insight 3
Brief intake: goals, non-negotiable constraints, definition of success in two years
Navigator maps goals against programs being considered, surfacing questions adults don't know to ask
Session ends with a concrete next step — an application, a financial aid office to contact, a program visit
Primary actors: CBOs, navigators, college advising offices
Upstream · Midstream · Downstream

Constraint-Aware Navigation

Flips the standard decision sequence by surfacing real-world constraints before presenting options — treating feasibility as a design input rather than an afterthought. Prevents wasted cognitive effort on options that were never viable.

Behavioral mechanism

Feasibility-first filtering. Cognitive effort goes toward comparing viable options, not eliminating impossible ones. Prevents late-stage discouragement.

Insight 3 Insight 4
Opens with hard parameters: hours per week, online/in-person, cost ceiling, timeline constraints
Only once parameters are set do options appear — and only those that fit within them
Works as a digital tool, advising intake, or CBO session structure
Primary actors: CBOs, navigators, digital tool developers, institutions. Flagged as a priority concept.
Downstream

Compare & Choose

Makes implicit tradeoffs explicit — presenting programs an adult is considering against decision-relevant dimensions: realistic cost, credential recognition, graduate outcomes at the program level, time to completion, and hidden requirements.

Behavioral mechanism

Tradeoff clarification. Makes the comparison adults are trying to make visible, rather than leaving it assembled from fragmented sources. Reduces reliance on misleading surface cues.

Insight 2 Insight 3
Adult identifies 2–3 programs they're genuinely considering; tool populates known data on each
Missing data is flagged explicitly — absence reads as a red flag, not a blank
Surfaces questions to ask rather than a single recommendation
Primary actors: CBOs, digital tool developers. Note: effectiveness depends on program-level data availability — a gap policy actors must address.
Upstream · Midstream · Downstream

Trusted Intermediaries

Embeds knowledgeable, trustworthy human support into the decision process through the channels adults already trust — not by creating new advisors, but by equipping people who already occupy trusted roles in adults' lives.

Behavioral mechanism

Channel effect. The same information, delivered through a trusted source, carries more weight and is more likely to be acted on than identical information through a low-trust channel.

Insight 1 Insight 2 Insight 4
CBOs train existing staff to run brief decision conversations in services adults already access
Employers with tuition support designate an HR contact who walks employees through options — not just a portal
Peer navigators who have personally completed similar programs are among the highest-leverage investments
Primary actors: CBOs, community organizations, employers, peer navigator programs. Flagged as a priority concept.
If You're Implementing This

Priorities by actor

The findings and design concepts have different implications depending on who you are and what levers you have access to. Select your context below.

CBOs & navigator programs

CBOs and navigators are uniquely positioned to act on this research because they already occupy the trusted intermediary role that our findings identify as central. Adults trust people they know, in channels they already use. The challenge is not convincing adults to seek out support — it is ensuring that the support available to them is equipped to address decision-stage needs, not just enrollment paperwork.

The most immediate priority is building decision support into existing touchpoints rather than creating new ones. Staff already in contact with adults navigating postsecondary decisions can be trained to conduct brief, structured decision conversations. This doesn't require a new program — it requires equipping existing staff with the right questions, the right information sources, and a simple framework for helping adults surface their constraints and compare their options.

For organizations that serve adults earlier in the decision process, the upstream version of Trusted Intermediaries is most relevant. Peer navigators who have personally completed programs in fields relevant to your population are among the highest-leverage investments this research points toward.

Priority concepts

Constraint-Aware Navigation and Guided Choice Sessions are most directly actionable — both can be embedded in advising workflows without significant technology investment. Trusted Intermediaries describes what CBOs do at their best: the implication is to systematize it and resource it as a core service.

Institutions

Institutions have two distinct opportunities, operating at different points in the decision process. The first is upstream: how institutions communicate about their programs before prospective students have committed to applying. Program websites and marketing materials consistently felt vague, promotional, and devoid of the information adults actually need. Employer recognition, realistic earnings at the program level, typical time to completion under real-world scheduling constraints, and what additional steps are required — these are the data points adults are looking for and consistently cannot find.

The second opportunity is at the point of decision: how institutions support prospective students actively comparing options. Most institutions are better resourced for enrolled students than for prospective ones. Shifting some advising capacity toward the pre-enrollment decision stage — particularly for adult learners less likely to have a counselor guiding them — is one of the most direct ways institutions can act on this research.

Where to start

It may be as simple as ensuring that an admissions conversation includes the constraint-mapping questions that Constraint-Aware Navigation describes, rather than jumping straight to application requirements. Institutions that make program-level outcome data available clearly and comparably will be more trusted and more likely to attract well-matched learners.

Policymakers

The most important thing policymakers can do is address the underlying data infrastructure problem that runs through every finding in this report. Adults cannot make well-informed choices in a system where program-level outcome data is fragmented, non-standardized, and frequently absent. Individual interventions can mitigate this gap at the margins. They cannot solve it. The data problem is systemic and requires a systemic response.

The research points toward three policy-level priorities: standardized outcome reporting at the program level; accountability standards for how programs represent their outcomes in marketing materials; and investment in the navigator and intermediary infrastructure that helps adults access and act on whatever information is available.

The enabling condition

The Compare & Choose concept depends on policy action to reach its potential. A tool that helps adults compare programs can only be as good as the data it draws on. Closing the program-level data gap is the enabling condition for most of the design work this report describes.

Funders

Funders are in a position to resource the navigation layer directly — in ways that cut across the institutional and organizational silos that currently make it difficult to build. The highest-leverage near-term opportunity is the downstream population: adults who have decided to pursue postsecondary education and are actively comparing options, but lack the support to choose well. This population is identifiable, their decision moment is bounded, and the relevant concepts are developed enough to pilot and test.

The upstream population — adults still working through whether school is the right move at all — presents a harder and more important challenge. The barriers are more diffuse, the entry points less obvious, and the most effective channel is human support through trusted relationships rather than tools or information. Investment in peer navigator programs and in building organizational capacity to provide decision support at scale is where the leverage is for this group.

Most durable investments

The most durable investments are in infrastructure: the data systems that make program-level outcomes visible, the training and tools that equip navigators to provide high-quality decision support, and the partnerships between institutions, CBOs, and community organizations that ensure support reaches adults where they already are.
